Designing Quant Systems for Change

QUANTITATIVE RESEARCH · SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE · BUSINESS WORKFLOW
Architecture and business workflow design for continuous, risk-controlled experimentation
Core idea

Keep likely changes local. Keep business definitions dependable. Make testing part of the design.

In systematic trading, we rarely know in advance which research ideas will make money. We therefore need to run many disciplined experiments, learn quickly, discard weak ideas, and move promising ones towards production under appropriate controls.

This talk is about the software and business-workflow design that helps us preserve that ability as systems grow. The central idea is simple: keep the parts that are likely to change behind dependable boundaries; keep the business concepts and operations used across those boundaries clear and stable enough to avoid ambiguity; and design testing into those same boundaries.

I will also connect these ideas to several long-running themes in software engineering—information hiding, program families, protected variations, seams, domain language, evolutionary design—and then ask how their value changes as AI coding tools take on more implementation and review.

Research agility is a competitive capability

Uncertain ideas

We cannot know in advance which research hypotheses will survive contact with data and markets.

Repeated experiments

Value comes from trying, measuring, learning and moving on—not from protecting every first idea.

Fast, controlled turnaround

The useful metric is time from idea to trustworthy evidence, and then to safe production when justified.

Architecture matters because it changes the cost and safety of the next experiment.

Agility is not a generic virtue here. It is closely tied to the economics of systematic trading. We work under uncertainty. A good hypothesis can fail; an unfashionable one can work; and something that works today may need to adapt as markets evolve.

So we need a research process that supports repeated, risk-controlled trial and error. The goal is not simply to write code quickly. It is to shorten the path from a research question to trustworthy evidence, and then, when the evidence is strong enough, to reliable production behaviour.

This is why the design of our research systems matters. A design that makes every new experiment require extensive integration, detective work or regression effort directly slows the business's ability to learn.

The first version of a research idea is often structurally simple. We are not necessarily more focused as people; the system itself has fewer existing relationships to respect. There may be one venue, one model, a small set of inputs and a straightforward graph.

Months later, the same area may have accumulated other users, shared state, additional data sources and special handling. A small feature can then require much more time than the original implementation.

The two-day and two-week figures are illustrative. The point is the shape of the problem: the cost of change can rise faster than the apparent size of the change. Our objective is not to eliminate all upfront speed. It is to prevent repeated experiments from becoming progressively harder.

The hidden cost is the experiment we decide not to run

A difficult change costs more than engineering time.

• A researcher drops an idea because the change looks too invasive.

• A useful comparison is postponed because the simulator setup is too fragile.

• A strategy stays unchanged because no one wants to reopen a tightly coupled graph.

Friction changes which research questions we are willing to ask.

The direct engineering cost is only part of the loss. If changing a system is unpleasant, researchers adapt their behaviour. Some ideas never become tickets. Some comparisons are postponed. Some old logic remains because touching it seems risky.

That matters in a business where the opportunity cost of not trying an idea can be significant. We will never observe those abandoned experiments in a project tracker, but the friction is real.

So the design question is larger than maintainability. Can our system preserve the team's appetite and ability to experiment?

Before we introduce our own interpretation, I want to step back briefly. The problem we are discussing has shaped system design for decades, and the ideas on this page are useful because each attacks a different part of the same problem.

Parnas's 1972 Information Hiding paper argues that a module should hide design decisions that are likely to change, rather than simply mirror the order of computation. His 1976 Program Families work then asks how a family of related programs can share a common structure while preserving the parts that genuinely vary.

Extreme Programming, published by Kent Beck in 1999, takes a delivery-oriented view: simple design, automated tests, refactoring, continuous integration and frequent delivery reinforce each other. Fowler's 2000 discussion of evolutionary design makes the complementary point that design can continue improving as part of delivery rather than being completed once at the beginning.

Larman's 2001 Protected Variations gives perhaps the most concise formulation for our purposes: identify the points where variation is expected and protect the rest of the system with stable interfaces. Evans's 2003 Domain-Driven Design broadens the discussion to the business domain: the language and representation in the system should capture the business distinctions that actually matter, while recognising that different business contexts may legitimately use different representations.

Feathers's 2004 seam model connects changeability directly to testability: a seam is a place where behaviour can be replaced or observed without editing unrelated code. Cockburn's 2005 Ports and Adapters architecture shows the same idea at a system boundary: production inputs, simulators and tests can drive the same core behaviour through stable ports.

I am not presenting these as a checklist of methodologies. The reason to show the history is that the same concern keeps reappearing: how do we let a system evolve without allowing every change to spread through everything else? Our next step is to apply that accumulated experience to quantitative trading business and systems.

This is the translation from software-design history into our quantitative-trading context.

We cannot know which model or business idea will make money, but we do know that some forms of variation are repeated features of the business: models change, venues change, products evolve, features are replaced, and business workflows are repeatedly recomposed.

So the goal is not maximum flexibility. The goal is to create useful seams around variation that we genuinely expect. At the technical level these may be APIs or subgraph boundaries. At the business level they are operations and workflow boundaries whose inputs, outputs, state ownership and effects are clear.

I prefer “keep unrelated parts independent” to “keep unrelated parts stable”. Stable can sound as if those parts should never change. The real point is that they should not have to change merely because this experiment or component changed.

State is compatible with modular design when it is owned and contained. Hidden state dependencies create coupling.

Stateful and coupled are not the same thing.

A predictor, estimator or business subgraph can own quite a lot of state and still be modular. The key question is whether that state is contained inside the boundary and whether its lifecycle and declared effects are explicit.

Coupling appears when other nodes quietly rely on internal timing, shared mutable state, reset behaviour or side effects that are not part of the contract. Then replacing one component requires coordinated edits elsewhere.

So the design test is not “is this node stateful?” It is “who owns the state, who is allowed to depend on it, and what must the rest of the graph know?”

The same design principle applies above the code level. We can think of a business workflow as a composition of operations. The operation definitions provide the dependable points of connection; the way we compose them can change as research, products or business requirements change.

That is why I do not want to separate technical architecture and business workflow architecture too sharply. They are two applications of the same principle: identify what varies, define what the rest of the system can rely on, and keep changes local where possible.

Let me make the business-workflow coupling concrete before we generalise it.

A first version of a basis trade might reasonably start from CME M- and Q-month contracts, calculate the spread, compare it with a three-sigma range, place a GTC order in the Q-month contract, and after that fills, use an IOC order in the M-month contract to establish the basis position.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this as a first implementation. The problem is that several independent business choices have become inseparable in the workflow: which underlying and active contracts we trade, how we represent the relative-value state, how we generate the signal, and how we execute the two legs.

Once research starts asking for different underlyings, different month conventions, another mean-reversion signal or another pair-execution tactic, the whole workflow becomes the unit of change.

The previous slide showed one highly specific CME workflow. Here I want to pull the camera back and show the larger business structure underneath it.

The first role is instrument and contract mapping. This matters because active-futures conventions differ across gold, crude, equity index and FX futures. Those month-code decisions should not be buried inside the signal or execution logic.

The next role is the relative-value state. A calendar basis, a cash-futures basis and a cointegration residual are not the same economic object. But they can play the same role in a workflow: they represent the relative relationship we want to monitor.

The signal model then decides whether that state represents an opportunity. Mean reversion is one family of signal logic; it is not part of the definition of basis itself.

Trade intent gives us a useful boundary between research logic and execution. The execution strategy then decides how to establish the position—for example by working the less-liquid leg passively and hedging the more-liquid leg once the first leg fills.

The four examples at the bottom are deliberately different. The point is not that they share identical economics or should use one generic implementation. The point is that we can recognise the same business roles while allowing each product to use its own mapping, state definition, signal model and execution implementation.

This gives us a reusable business structure without erasing genuine product differences. It also gives us clearer places to experiment, test and extend the system.

Avoid two common extremes

Copy every workflow

Fast once, but fixes and improvements diverge; comparison becomes harder.

Build one universal framework

Flags, conditions and accidental interactions accumulate before the common structure is understood.

Share what has a stable common contract. Keep genuine differences explicit.

A change-oriented design does not imply maximum reuse. The two obvious extremes are repeated copying and premature generalisation.

Copying can be a sensible short-term research choice, especially when we do not yet understand whether two ideas are genuinely related. But repeated copies become expensive if they evolve together. On the other hand, a universal framework built too early can become a collection of conditionals and flags.

The practical rule is to abstract around stable shared contracts and observed variation, not visual similarity alone.

Understand the business before designing models and systems

Business conceptsWhat are the underlying facts, states and distinctions that actually matter to the trading business?
Business operationsWhat can be done, under which conditions, and what state or external world may each operation change?
Models and systemsRepresent those concepts and operations clearly enough that workflows can be composed without guessing or recreating lost distinctions downstream.
Models and systems should be true to the business — but that requires understanding the underlying concepts and workflows, not simply copying today's process or taxonomy.

This section goes one step further than saying that system objects should have good business names. Before we design the models and systems, we need to understand what the business concepts and operations really are.

That can require a fairly deep domain discussion. We need to understand the facts and states that matter, the operations the business performs, their applicability, and the effects they are allowed to have. Only then can we decide which distinctions our models and systems need to preserve.

Domain-Driven Design is useful here as a reference because it treats rigorous domain understanding and shared language as part of system design. But the point for us is practical rather than methodological: if our understanding of the business is vague or fragmented, the system will faithfully encode that vagueness and fragmentation.

And that leads to the next point. The business taxonomy and workflow we see today may themselves be the product of years of organic development. We need to distinguish the underlying business from the historical path by which today's process emerged.

Do not simplify by cutting business corners. Simplify by understanding which complexity is fundamental and which is an artefact of history, organisation or earlier process choices.

Trading businesses rarely emerge from a clean first-principles taxonomy. They grow in stages. Products are added at different times, desks solve local problems, processes inherit earlier constraints, and terminology reflects the organisation that existed when each piece was created.

If we simply copy that history into our models and systems, we can preserve every local patch as a separate object, flag or workflow. The system then inherits the fragmentation of the business's development path rather than representing the underlying business clearly.

Sometimes the right design work therefore starts with a first-principles review of the business itself. We need to understand why the current categories and processes exist, which distinctions are economically or operationally real, and which are historical artefacts. A deeper review may show that several apparently different cases are expressions of one underlying concept; in other places it may show that one convenient label is hiding two genuinely different business states.

The qualification is important: we must remain true to the business. This is not permission to simplify away a real trading constraint because a cleaner system would be convenient. The simplification is justified only when deeper understanding shows that the fragmentation is accidental rather than fundamental.

Once we have a clearer understanding of the business, the objects and states in our models and systems should preserve the distinctions that actually matter to it.

A familiar market-data example is the difference between a complete state and an update whose meaning depends on previous state. Another is the difference between a genuine business instruction to target zero and the absence of a new instruction. The exact examples are less important than the pattern.

If the system representation collapses two business states that need different operations, downstream nodes have to reconstruct the lost distinction through flags, context and special cases. Complexity has leaked from an imprecise upstream model into every consumer.

Conversely, we should not preserve distinctions merely because the current business process happens to use two historical names. The distinction should survive in software when it is genuinely needed by the business behaviour.

Define operations by business intent and explicit effects

Business intent

What business action or decision does this operation represent? Its name and result should match that intention precisely.

Applicability

When is the operation valid to run? What input, business state, timing or lifecycle conditions must hold?

Effects

What state or external action may it change? Those effects should be explicit; unrelated state should not change unexpectedly.

Clear operations remain modular building blocks as the system grows. New modules and features can be added with a much clearer view of their effect on existing behaviour.

An operation should reflect the business intention as exactly as we reasonably can. That means its purpose, applicability, output and effects are easy to understand from the business point of view.

I do not mean that every operation must be side-effect free. Sending an order clearly has an external effect. Reserving risk capacity may deliberately mutate state. The requirement is that those effects are intended, explicit and owned—rather than surprising side effects that other components only discover indirectly.

This matters when the system grows. If each operation remains a clear modular unit, we can add a new feature, factor an existing workflow, or introduce another module with a much better understanding of what existing behaviour can and cannot be affected.

That is one of the strongest reasons to invest in precise business definitions: they make future extension safer, not because the system stops changing, but because the impact of change is easier to reason about.

Some edge cases come from reality; others come from fragmented definitions and history

Genuine external exception

This distinction is now more nuanced than simply reality versus system.

Some edge cases are genuinely imposed by the external world. If a rare market or operational event is real, easy to detect and safely contained, a simple guard or recovery path may be exactly the right solution.

But some apparent business edge cases are products of history. A business may have developed in stages, with local taxonomies and processes that made sense at different moments. Once we understand the underlying business more deeply, several of those cases may turn out to be different historical expressions of the same concept.

And a third category is ambiguity introduced by the system representation itself: we collapse two genuinely different states, then downstream code has to reconstruct the distinction through special cases.

The engineering response should depend on which category we are dealing with. The purpose of first-principles business modelling is not to deny business complexity. It is to avoid treating historical or modelling fragmentation as if it were an irreducible law of the business.

Bounded Context is a useful DDD concept here because it prevents the opposite misunderstanding: accurate definitions do not mean the whole organisation needs one universal vocabulary or representation.

The same word can legitimately have different representations in different business contexts. A research signal and an execution instruction may be related but should not be forced into one generic type if their semantics differ.

The goal is consistency within a context and explicit translation between contexts where needed. That gives us precision without building a universal framework that erases important distinctions.

What hidden assumptions can cost

Interface values were interpreted with inconsistent units.
Mars Climate Orbiter
CAUSE

A local change interacted with hidden legacy behaviour.
Knight Capital
CAUSE
Knight introduced new trading functionality that reused a control flag previously associated with dormant Power Peg code. These three incidents follow the same structure: what the issue looked like, what caused it, what it cost, and the lesson relevant to our design discussion.

Mars Climate Orbiter is the cleanest example of a definition mismatch. A contractor's ground software expressed thruster impulse in pound-force seconds. The navigation software treated the incoming values as newton-seconds. Nothing about the numeric values themselves announced that mismatch. Each correction therefore distorted the estimated trajectory until the spacecraft arrived at Mars on the wrong path and was lost. The lesson for us is that compatibility depends on a shared definition, not merely on a field type or a plausible number.

Ariane 5 illustrates inherited assumptions. The inertial-reference software was reused from Ariane 4, including a calculation built around the older vehicle's expected horizontal-velocity range. Ariane 5 had a different trajectory and acceleration profile. A value exceeded that inherited range, a conversion failed, and the two redundant inertial systems shared the same implementation and therefore the same failure. Reuse is valuable, but the assumptions under which an implementation is valid remain part of its contract.

Knight Capital is especially relevant to trading. A new feature reused a control flag that had previously activated dormant Power Peg functionality. The new release was deployed to seven of eight servers. On the eighth server the old code remained, so the same flag activated obsolete order-generation behaviour. That local deployment inconsistency interacted with hidden legacy behaviour and produced more than 460 million dollars of losses in roughly 45 minutes.

I would not claim that one architecture pattern would have prevented these incidents. Their value here is narrower: they show how definition mismatch, inherited assumptions and hidden dependencies can turn changes that look local into system-level consequences.

Testability is an architectural property

A useful seam is also a useful test boundary.

• Stable inputs and outputs give us reusable contract tests.

• Swappable variants give us clear expected differences.

• Contained state makes reset and reproducibility tractable.

• Full graph replay checks the interaction of all components.

This is where Michael Feathers's seam becomes more than a metaphor. A seam is useful because we can replace behaviour, but that also means we have a natural place to drive, observe and verify behaviour.

Stable boundaries make test design clearer. We can test the contract once across compatible implementations, then add feature-specific tests for the intended differences, and finally run system-level replay to verify composition.

This matters later for AI coding as well. The path from manual review to more automated review depends heavily on having verification that is explicit, repeatable and independent of one developer's understanding of the code.

This is where Extreme Programming provides a useful historical reference. The important principle for us is not a particular XP ritual. It is the idea that improving the design is a continuous part of delivering software.

That fits our environment well. We do not need a complete architecture for every hypothetical future strategy. We can start simply, learn from real variation, and improve the seams and definitions as the system becomes shared infrastructure.

But simply shipping frequent patches is not evolutionary design. If every delivery adds another special case without improving the underlying structure, the ability to change still deteriorates.

This is the Design Stamina idea in plain language.

A quick local change may genuinely be the best way to get the first result. The trade-off appears when the same part of the system changes repeatedly. If every variation requires us to rediscover dependencies, reconnect several components and rebuild the regression story, the accumulated cost can become much larger than the first implementation suggested.

A useful boundary and reusable tests cost something to establish, but they can reduce that repeated work. The point is not that the second approach always wins. It is to judge the likely lifetime pattern of change rather than looking only at today's implementation effort.

Fowler called this the Design Stamina Hypothesis because it is a hypothesis to evaluate, not a law that justifies architecture everywhere.

If something is probably a one-off research experiment, keeping it simple may be exactly right. If a rare external event can be detected and contained safely, a guard and alert may be better than building a large framework around it.

Designing for change sustains research agility

Keep likely changes localInformation Hiding · Protected Variations · Seams
Understand and model the business deliberatelydeep domain understanding · simplify historical fragmentation where justified · preserve real business distinctions
Design testability into the architecturereusable contracts · simulator · regression

Good architecture is not the objective. Sustained, risk-controlled research agility is.

Let me finish where we started. In systematic trading, we cannot know in advance which research ideas will succeed. What we can build is the capability to keep trying good ideas, test them reliably, learn from the evidence and move successful ideas towards production without rebuilding the system every time.

The first principle is to keep likely changes local. Use dependable seams around technical components and business operations that we expect to vary repeatedly.

The second is broader than simply making software fit today's business terminology. We need to understand the business deeply enough to model its underlying concepts and operations. Sometimes that means faithfully preserving a real distinction. Sometimes it means recognising that today's taxonomy is the product of staged, organic development and can be simplified after a first-principles review. We must not simplify by cutting business corners; the software should remain true to the business we actually intend to operate.

Third, treat testability as an architectural property. Stable boundaries give us reusable contracts and a production-faithful simulator gives us repeatable evidence about the combined workflow.

And fourth, improving the design is part of delivery. The boundaries and business models we choose are not permanent truths; they should evolve as we learn, with proportionate engineering judgement.

As AI takes on more coding and review, these ideas move from helping people manage implementation complexity towards supporting automated, controlled and cumulative experimentation.

Good architecture is not the objective. Sustained research agility is.
